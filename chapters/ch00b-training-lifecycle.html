Training Lifecycle: How a Model Like Claude Gets Made

General AI Eng pretraining → fine-tuning → RLHF, explained without the math

Why this matters. You will never personally train a model like Claude — that costs hundreds of millions of dollars and needs data-center-scale hardware. But knowing the three stages it went through explains a lot of its behavior: why it "knows" so much, why it follows instructions instead of just rambling, and why it refuses certain requests. This is 100% background knowledge, not a skill you'll practice — it's here so the rest of the curriculum makes sense.

The analogy for this whole chapter

Think of training a model like raising and educating a brilliant but very naive person:
  1. Pretraining = reading almost the entire internet, cover to cover, absorbing patterns, facts, and how language works — but with no one telling them what's true, helpful, or appropriate.
  2. Fine-tuning (SFT) = a tutor sitting with them, showing worked examples: "here's a good question, and here's how a helpful assistant should answer it."
  3. RLHF / preference tuning = getting feedback on their own answers ("this response was better than that one") and adjusting their behavior to match what people actually prefer.

1. Pretraining — the expensive, general part

The model starts as random noise — it knows nothing. It's shown trillions of words of text (books, websites, code, papers) and given one simple job over and over: predict the next token. When it guesses wrong, its internal numbers (called "weights") get nudged slightly to make the correct answer a bit more likely next time. Repeat that trillions of times and it slowly learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and coding — purely as a side effect of getting extremely good at next-token prediction.

// The core training loop, wildly simplified
for (const batch of trainingData) {         // billions of batches
  const prediction = model.predictNext(batch.input);
  const loss = compareToActualNextToken(prediction, batch.actualNext);
  model.weights = nudgeWeightsToReduceLoss(model.weights, loss);
}
// After enough repetitions → the "base model" / "pretrained model"

The result of this stage alone is called a base model. It's genuinely smart, but it isn't well-behaved — ask it a question and it might continue with more questions (because that's a common pattern in its training data) instead of answering. It has no concept of "be a helpful assistant to this specific user."

2. Fine-tuning (SFT) — teaching it to be an assistant

SFT = Supervised Fine-Tuning. Humans (or high-quality AI-generated examples) write out thousands of examples of good instruction-following behavior: "Question → ideal answer" pairs. The model is trained again — much more lightly than pretraining — on just this curated data, so it learns the pattern "when someone asks me something, respond helpfully and directly," instead of just continuing text.

Pretraining data (internet-scale, messy): "...the capital of france is often a trivia question, and other european capitals include..." SFT data (curated, example-driven): User: "What's the capital of France?" Assistant: "The capital of France is Paris." ← model learns THIS shape

This is the stage that turns a raw text-predictor into something that feels like it's talking to you.

3. RLHF / preference tuning — polishing based on what humans actually like

RLHF = Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. At this point the model can follow instructions, but its answers might still be too long, too short, unclear, or subtly unsafe. So:

  1. The model generates multiple different answers to the same prompt.
  2. Humans (or a trained "reward model" standing in for human judgment) rank which answer is better.
  3. The model is nudged to produce more answers like the ones ranked highly, and fewer like the ones ranked poorly.
Prompt: "Explain photosynthesis" Answer A: [3 clear paragraphs with an analogy] 👍 ranked better Answer B: [a wall of dense jargon, no structure] 👎 ranked worse → model weights nudged to produce more "Answer A"-style responses

This stage is also where a lot of safety behavior gets shaped — refusing harmful requests, avoiding certain topics, being careful with sensitive advice. Anthropic's specific approach also uses techniques like Constitutional AI, where the model critiques and improves its own answers against a written set of principles, reducing how much raw human labeling is needed. You don't need the details — just know that "why did Claude refuse that" or "why did Claude phrase it so carefully" traces back to this stage, not the pretraining stage.

4. Making models smaller & cheaper: LoRA and quantization

The three stages above produce a big, full-precision model. Two more techniques show up constantly once people want to adapt or run models cheaply — especially open-weight models like Llama or Mistral. You won't apply these to Claude (Anthropic serves it for you), but the terms come up everywhere, so know what they mean.

LoRA — cheap fine-tuning (adapt without retraining everything)

LoRA = Low-Rank Adaptation. Full fine-tuning updates all of a model's billions of weights — slow, expensive, and it produces a whole new copy of the model. LoRA instead freezes the original weights and trains a tiny set of extra "adapter" numbers alongside them (often less than 1% of the model's size). You get most of the benefit of fine-tuning for a fraction of the cost and storage.

Think of the base model as a printed textbook you're not allowed to rewrite. Full fine-tuning = reprinting the entire book to change a few chapters. LoRA = leaving the book untouched and clipping in a thin packet of sticky-note corrections. Cheap to make, easy to swap out, and you can keep many different note-packets for different tasks.

QLoRA is just LoRA applied on top of a quantized (compressed) base model — so you can even fine-tune large models on a single consumer GPU. It's the reason hobbyists can fine-tune open models at home at all.

Quantization — cheap serving (shrink a trained model)

A model's weights are normally stored as high-precision numbers (e.g. 16-bit floats). Quantization stores them at lower precision — 8-bit or even 4-bit integers — so the model takes far less memory and runs faster, with only a small drop in quality.

Full precision (16-bit): 0.8123456 ← accurate, but 2 bytes each × billions Quantized (8-bit): 0.81 ← "rounded", 1 byte each → ½ the memory Quantized (4-bit): 0.8 ← rounded harder, ¼ the memory, a bit lossy

The trade-off is the familiar one: smaller + faster + cheaper vs. slightly lower quality. 8-bit is usually nearly lossless; 4-bit saves the most but you can start to feel it on hard tasks.

When does this matter to you? Only if you self-host an open-weight model. With the Claude API you never quantize or LoRA anything — Anthropic runs the model and you just call it. This is also the deeper answer to "use a smaller/cheaper model" back in the cost chapter (Ch. 20): quantization and small fine-tuned models are how open-model providers offer cheap tiers. Where you fit in

As an AI engineer using the Claude API, you never touch any of these three stages — Anthropic already did them, and you're consuming the finished model. But you do influence behavior at a much lighter level, without retraining anything, and it's worth seeing how these map:

None of these change the model's weights — they just steer the already-trained model's behavior for your specific use case. That's most of what "prompt engineering" (Chapter 13) really is.

Key takeaways

  • Pretraining: massive, expensive, next-token prediction on huge text corpora — produces a "base model" that's knowledgeable but not well-behaved.
  • SFT (fine-tuning): lighter training on curated instruction→answer examples, teaches the model to act like a helpful assistant.
  • RLHF / preference tuning: humans rank multiple answers, model is nudged toward the preferred style — this is also where most safety behavior comes from.
  • As an API user, you don't retrain anything — you steer the finished model with system prompts, examples, and tools instead.
  • This explains real behavior: refusals and careful phrasing = RLHF; broad knowledge = pretraining; instruction-following = SFT.
  • LoRA = cheap fine-tuning (train a small add-on, freeze the rest); quantization = cheap serving (store weights at lower precision). Both trade a little quality for big savings — and only matter if you self-host open models.

Understanding check
  1. What does the model learn to do during pretraining? Predict the next token over massive amounts of text — this is where most of its "knowledge" and language ability comes from.
  2. What problem does SFT solve that pretraining alone doesn't? A pretrained base model just continues text in whatever pattern is statistically likely; SFT teaches it the specific "answer helpfully as an assistant" behavior.
  3. Where does most of a model's safety/refusal behavior come from? RLHF / preference tuning, where human (or reward-model) feedback ranks answers and nudges the model toward preferred, safer responses.
  4. What's the difference between LoRA and quantization? LoRA makes fine-tuning cheap (train a small adapter, freeze the original weights); quantization makes running the model cheap (store weights at lower precision). One adapts, the other shrinks.
