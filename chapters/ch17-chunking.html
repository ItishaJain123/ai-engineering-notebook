Chunking Strategies

General AI Eng splitting documents into pieces small enough to embed and precise enough to retrieve

Why this matters. Chapter 21 showed how to turn text into a vector so meaning-similar text ends up numerically close. But you can't hand a 50-page PDF to an embedding model as one unit — most models have a max input length, and even if they didn't, one giant vector representing an entire document is useless for retrieval: it can't tell you which paragraph answers the user's question. So before anything gets embedded, it first gets chunked — split into smaller, self-contained pieces. Chunking quality is one of the biggest levers on RAG accuracy, often bigger than which embedding model you pick.

🟢 In plain words. You can't embed a whole 50-page document as one blob — it's too big, and one vector for the whole thing can't point at the right paragraph. Chunking is cutting the document into small, self-contained pieces so search can return just the passage that answers the question.
🔍 The tricky part, in plain language. You can't embed a whole 50-page document as one vector — you'd blur every topic into a single averaged "mush" point that matches nothing well. So you cut the document into chunks and embed each one separately. The hard question is where to cut: too big and each chunk covers many topics (fuzzy matches); too small and you slice a single idea in half, so neither piece makes sense alone. Picture cutting a movie into scenes — cut at scene boundaries and each clip is coherent; cut blindly every 10 seconds and you get half-sentences. That's why you chunk on natural boundaries (paragraphs, headings) and add a little overlap so ideas aren't severed. Takeaway: chunking decides what a "unit of meaning" is — get it wrong and even a perfect embedding model retrieves junk.

Real-world analogy

Think about indexing a book. You wouldn't hand a reader the entire book and say "the answer is in here somewhere" — you'd break it into chapters, then sections, then paragraphs, so a reader (or a search index) can point to the exact passage that answers a question. Cut the book into pieces too large (whole chapters) and the reader still has to hunt. Cut it too small (individual words) and each piece loses all context. Chunking is deciding where to make the cuts so each piece is both findable and still makes sense on its own — like slicing a cake so every slice has some frosting, not just crumbs from a mid-slice cut.

1. Why chunking has to happen before embedding

Retrieval works by comparing a query's vector against many stored vectors and returning the closest ones (Ch. 21). If each vector represents an entire document, the best you can retrieve is "this whole 50-page document is somewhat relevant" — not useful when a user needs one specific fact. Chunking splits the document into smaller units first, embeds each chunk separately, and stores them individually. Now retrieval can return just the two or three paragraphs that actually answer the question, instead of dumping the whole document into the model's context window.

2. The main chunking strategies

StrategyHow it worksTradeoff
Fixed-sizeSplit every N characters or tokens, regardless of contentSimple and fast, but happily cuts mid-sentence or mid-word — cheapest to implement, lowest quality
Sentence / paragraph-basedSplit on natural sentence or paragraph boundariesKeeps ideas intact, but chunk sizes become uneven — a one-line paragraph and a 40-line paragraph both become "one chunk"
Recursive character splittingTry splitting on paragraphs first; if a piece is still too big, fall back to sentences; if still too big, fall back to raw charactersThe practical default in most RAG frameworks — balances respecting structure with guaranteeing a max size
Semantic chunkingEmbed sentences, then cut where meaning shifts significantly (using embedding similarity between consecutive sentences)Produces the most coherent chunks, but costs extra embedding calls just to decide where to cut — slower and pricier
Document-structure-awareRespect the document's own structure — markdown headers, code blocks, table boundaries — never splitting inside themBest for technical docs, READMEs, and code; requires format-specific parsing logic per document type

3. Overlap: why chunks usually share some text

If you cut chunks with zero overlap, a sentence (or the connective idea between two sentences) that straddles a chunk boundary gets silently broken — the end of chunk 1 and the start of chunk 2 each lose context the other one needed. The common fix is overlap: each chunk repeats roughly the last 10-20% of the previous chunk, so boundary information survives in at least one complete chunk.

Text: "...the refund policy allows returns within 30 days of purchase, provided the item is unused and in its original packaging. Exceptions apply to..." Without overlap: Chunk 1: "...the refund policy allows returns within 30 days of purchase, provided" Chunk 2: "the item is unused and in its original packaging. Exceptions apply to..." → neither chunk alone states the full condition clearly With ~15% overlap: Chunk 1: "...the refund policy allows returns within 30 days of purchase, provided the item is unused and in its original packaging." Chunk 2: "...provided the item is unused and in its original packaging. Exceptions apply to..." → the boundary sentence appears whole in at least one chunk

4. The core tradeoff: chunk size

Chunk sizeProblem
Too smallEach chunk loses surrounding context (a sentence like "it costs $50" is meaningless without knowing what "it" is); you also end up with far more chunks to search through and store.
Too largeThe chunk's embedding blurs together multiple unrelated ideas, diluting relevance for any single query; when retrieved, a large chunk wastes context window space (Ch. 20's cost/latency tradeoffs apply directly here); it may also exceed the embedding model's max input length (Ch. 21), causing silent truncation.

There's no universal "correct" size — it depends on the embedding model's sweet spot, the document type, and how much surrounding context a good answer typically needs. Many teams start around 300-800 tokens per chunk with ~15% overlap, then tune based on retrieval quality.

5. A simple chunker, in JavaScript

Here's an illustrative recursive-ish splitter: it tries to break on paragraph boundaries first, and only falls back to a fixed-size character cut (with overlap) when a paragraph is still too big.

function chunkText(text, { maxChars = 800, overlap = 120 } = {}) {
  const paragraphs = text.split(/\n\s*\n/);  // try paragraph boundaries first
  const chunks = [];
  let current = "";

  for (const para of paragraphs) {
    if ((current + "\n\n" + para).length <= maxChars) {
      current = current ? current + "\n\n" + para : para;
    } else {
      if (current) chunks.push(current);
      // fall back to fixed-size splitting if a single paragraph is too big
      if (para.length > maxChars) {
        chunks.push(...splitFixedSize(para, maxChars, overlap));
        current = "";
      } else {
        current = para;
      }
    }
  }
  if (current) chunks.push(current);
  return chunks;
}

function splitFixedSize(text, maxChars, overlap) {
  const pieces = [];
  let start = 0;
  while (start < text.length) {
    const end = Math.min(start + maxChars, text.length);
    pieces.push(text.slice(start, end));
    if (end === text.length) break;
    start = end - overlap;  // step back so the next chunk overlaps this one
  }
  return pieces;
}
Production note. Real-world libraries (e.g. LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter) generalize this idea with a whole list of fallback separators — try "\n\n", then "\n", then ". ", then " ", then raw characters — and measure size in tokens rather than characters, since that's what actually counts against an embedding model's max input length.

6. Common mistakes

  • Chunking mid-sentence or mid-code-block — a fixed-size cut that lands in the middle of a function definition or a sentence produces a chunk that's nonsensical on its own, hurting both embedding quality and readability if it's shown to a user.
  • No overlap — information sitting right at a chunk boundary gets silently split across two chunks, and neither one alone is likely to match a relevant query.
  • One chunk size for every document type — dense code, flowing prose, and structured tables all need different treatment; a chunk size tuned for narrative text will mangle a table or a code file, and vice versa.

Key takeaways

  • Documents are chunked before embedding so retrieval can return a specific relevant passage instead of an entire document.
  • Strategies range from simple (fixed-size) to structure-aware (recursive, semantic, markdown/code-aware) — recursive splitting is the common practical default.
  • Overlap (~10-20%) prevents context loss at chunk boundaries by letting boundary text appear fully in at least one chunk.
  • Chunk size is a real tradeoff: too small loses context and multiplies chunks to search; too large dilutes relevance, wastes context window, and risks exceeding the embedding model's max input length.
  • Chunking strategy should match the document type — code, prose, and tables each deserve different handling.

Understanding check
  1. Why can't you just embed an entire 50-page document as a single vector for retrieval? Retrieval works by matching a query's vector against stored vectors — a single vector for the whole document can only signal "this whole document is somewhat relevant," not point to the specific passage that answers the question, and it may also exceed the embedding model's max input length.
  2. What problem does chunk overlap solve, and what's a typical overlap percentage? Without overlap, a sentence or idea that straddles a chunk boundary gets split so that neither chunk alone contains it fully. Overlapping by roughly 10-20% ensures boundary content appears whole in at least one chunk.
💬 Conceptual questions (the kind interviewers ask)

Answer each out loud before peeking. These tie this chapter to real production concerns — cost, latency, failures, and quality.

  1. People say chunking often matters more than which embedding model you pick. Why?
    If a chunk splits one idea in half or mixes several unrelated ideas together, no embedding model can retrieve it cleanly — it's garbage in. Chunk boundaries decide what "a retrievable unit of meaning" even is, so they cap the whole system's accuracy.
  2. How do you choose chunk size, and what fails at each extreme?
    Too small loses context ("it costs $50" — what's "it"?) and multiplies the vectors you store and search; too large blurs multiple ideas into one vector, wastes context-window space, and can exceed the embedding model's max input (silent truncation). Start around 300–800 tokens with ~15% overlap, then tune on retrieval quality.
  3. Why do chunks usually overlap, and what does zero overlap cost you?
    A fact sitting on a boundary gets split so neither chunk contains it fully and neither matches the query. Roughly 10–20% overlap keeps boundary content whole in at least one chunk.
  4. You use one chunk size for code, prose, and tables alike. What goes wrong?
    A size tuned for prose slices code mid-function and cuts tables mid-row, producing nonsensical chunks that hurt retrieval. Match the strategy to the document type — structure-aware splitting for code and markdown.
