RAG vs. Fine-tuning vs. Prompting

General AI Eng a decision framework for picking the cheapest option that actually solves the problem

Why this matters. By now you have three big tools for getting an LLM to behave the way you want or know what it needs to know: prompting (Ch. 13-20), RAG (Ch. 21-25), and fine-tuning (mentioned back in Ch. 2 as part of the training pipeline). Beginners often reach for the most complex option first — fine-tuning sounds impressive — when a better prompt would have solved the problem in five minutes. This capstone chapter is a decision framework for choosing correctly.

🟢 In plain words. Three ways to get a model to do what you want: prompting (change your words — free and instant), RAG (give it a filing cabinet of facts to look things up in), and fine-tuning (retrain it so a behavior is baked in — slow and pricey). Try them in that order.
🔍 The tricky part, in plain language. The three options fix different problems, and people mix them up. Prompting changes the instructions (cheap, instant — but the model still only knows what's in the prompt). RAG changes the knowledge by looking things up at answer-time (great when facts change often — you update the documents, not the model). Fine-tuning changes the behavior/style by retraining on examples (great for consistent tone or format, but expensive, and stale the moment the facts change). Picture a new employee: prompting = clearer instructions, RAG = handing them a searchable manual, fine-tuning = sending them to weeks of training. Takeaway: changing facts → RAG; changing style → fine-tuning; changing instructions → prompting — and it's very common to combine prompting + RAG.

Real-world analogy

Think of a new employee at a company. Prompting is giving them clear instructions for one task — "write this email in a formal tone, keep it under 200 words." RAG is giving them a reference manual and a filing cabinet they can look things up in whenever they need a fact they don't have memorized. Fine-tuning is sending them through months of specialized retraining so a skill or style becomes second nature, baked into how they instinctively work. Each has a real cost — instructions are free and instant, the filing cabinet takes some setup, months of retraining is a major investment — and each is the right call in a different situation. You wouldn't send someone through months of retraining just to answer one question they could've looked up.

1. What each tool actually changes

The key to choosing correctly is understanding that these three tools change fundamentally different things about the system.

  • Prompting changes nothing about the model itself — you're steering behavior per-request through the words you send. It's the cheapest option, the fastest to iterate on (edit text, try again), and needs no persistent storage or infrastructure.
  • RAG gives the model access to information it doesn't have memorized — fresh, private, or too-large-to-fit-in-training data, retrieved at query time (Ch. 21-25). It doesn't change the model's behavior or style at all — only what facts it can reference when answering.
  • Fine-tuning actually adjusts the model's weights on a custom dataset — it changes behavior, style, or output format in a way that's baked in, not just what facts are available. It's the most expensive option, the slowest to iterate (retrain, re-evaluate, redeploy), and requires a real labeled training dataset.
Prompting: same model weights, same knowledge → different words in → different behavior out RAG: same model weights, same base behavior → + retrieved facts → answers grounded in new info Fine-tuning: DIFFERENT model weights → same words in → different behavior, baked in

4. The practical order most teams should try things in

1. Prompting first. Cheap, fast, and often sufficient on its own (Ch. 13). Most "the model won't do X" problems are solved by a clearer, more specific prompt — few-shot examples, explicit format instructions, or a better system prompt.

2. Add RAG if the failure is "doesn't know this specific fact or document." If the model is confidently wrong or has no way to know something (Ch. 21-25), that's a retrieval problem, not a prompting or training problem.

3. Only consider fine-tuning if failures persist and are about consistent behavior, format, or style that extensive prompting and RAG genuinely couldn't fix. And even then — try it only after you've quantified the failure rate you're trying to fix, so you can tell afterward whether the expensive investment actually helped.

5. They're not mutually exclusive

These tools stack. Production systems very commonly combine prompting + RAG together — a well-crafted system prompt that also has retrieved context injected into it. That combination alone handles the vast majority of real-world use cases. Fine-tuning is comparatively rare in production because prompting and RAG solve most problems for a fraction of the cost — reach for it only when you have clear evidence the first two aren't enough.

Key takeaways

  • Prompting changes nothing about the model — it steers behavior per-request through words alone. Cheapest and fastest to iterate.
  • RAG gives the model facts it doesn't have memorized, retrieved at query time — it doesn't change the model's style or behavior, only what it can reference.
  • Fine-tuning adjusts the model's weights — it changes behavior/style/format persistently, but is the most expensive and slowest option, and needs real training data.
  • The practical order is: try prompting first, add RAG for missing/fresh facts, and only consider fine-tuning for persistent behavior/format problems that prompting and RAG couldn't fix.
  • These aren't mutually exclusive — prompting + RAG together is the common production pattern; fine-tuning is comparatively rare.

Understanding check
  1. What's the fundamental difference between what RAG changes versus what fine-tuning changes? RAG only changes what facts the model can reference at query time — the model's underlying behavior and style stay the same. Fine-tuning actually adjusts the model's weights, changing its behavior, style, or output format persistently, not just the facts available to it.
  2. Why should a team try prompting before jumping to fine-tuning, even if they're fairly confident fine-tuning will be needed eventually? Prompting is far cheaper and faster to iterate on, and often solves the problem outright — jumping straight to fine-tuning risks a costly, slow investment in a fix that a much simpler and cheaper approach might have already handled.
💬 Conceptual questions (the kind interviewers ask)

This is a decision-making chapter, and interviewers love decision questions. Answer each out loud before peeking.

  1. The model keeps citing an outdated return policy that changes weekly. Which tool, and why not fine-tuning?
    RAG — it's a missing/fresh-fact problem, and RAG lets you re-index anytime. Fine-tuning bakes facts in at training time, so they'd go stale immediately and need a full retrain every week.
  2. You need a consistent empathetic tone across thousands of replies and prompting hasn't nailed it. Which tool?
    Consider fine-tuning — persistent behavior/style/format is exactly what it changes. But first quantify the failure rate and exhaust strong prompting/few-shot, so you can actually tell whether the expensive investment helped.
  3. Rank the three by cost and iteration speed — and why does that ordering matter?
    Prompting is cheapest and iterates in seconds; RAG is moderate setup, minutes to hours; fine-tuning is high cost, hours to days, and needs labeled data. Trying the cheaper, faster levers first means you fail fast and rarely need the expensive one.
  4. What's the most common production pattern, and why is fine-tuning comparatively rare?
    Prompting + RAG together — a good system prompt with retrieved context injected — handles the large majority of use cases cheaply. Fine-tuning is rare because the first two solve most problems for a fraction of the cost; reach for it only with clear evidence they weren't enough.
